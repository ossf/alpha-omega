# September 2026

## Private Beta for Organizations

The Organizations beta gained [gem-name reservations](https://github.com/rubygems/rubygems.org/pull/6777), allowing organizations to protect available names before publishing. This supports companies that want to prevent public packages from being published under names they use internally, reducing a source of dependency-confusion risk. We are taking a controlled approach to access while working through policies on limits and fairness, so reservations do not become a mechanism for broad name squatting.  

We [restored push and yank notifications for organization members](https://github.com/rubygems/rubygems.org/pull/6745). Moving gems into an organization had removed the emails members previously received through individual ownership. Confirmed members can now receive those notifications and control their preferences, restoring visibility into releases and unexpected publishing activity. A separate fix made gem transfers work when co-owners were already members of the destination organization.

We removed a permissions gap that prevented organization owners and administrators from [managing trusted publishers](https://github.com/rubygems/rubygems.org/pull/6833) for their gems. This makes it practical for organizations to use CI-based publishing without relying on long-lived API keys or individual ownership workarounds. We also moved [protected-name typo checks](https://github.com/rubygems/rubygems.org/pull/6330) into the setup of pending trusted publishers. Names that are too similar to protected gems are now rejected at configuration time, giving maintainers useful feedback before their first release fails.

## Package Repository Security

### OpenAI Swarm research

September brought renewed scrutiny of the May spam-publishing campaign after Nightingale Collective published research attributing the activity to OpenAI agents. The team reviewed the researchers’ evidence, met with OpenAI, coordinated with the maintainers of RubyDoc.info, and responded to press inquiries. We published [an update](https://blog.rubygems.org/2026/09/11/update-may-spam-publishing-campaign.html) explaining what our investigation could establish: the packages included attempts to obtain other users’ API keys, but we found no evidence that those attempts succeeded, and could not independently determine whether AI agents created or published the packages. This work took time away from planned development, but helped give the community a clear account of the incident and reinforced the need for better evidence retention and abuse detection.

### Advanced content addressable gems

Content addressable gems moved into wider testing with their inclusion in the [RubyGems and Bundler 4.1 beta](https://blog.rubygems.org/2026/09/09/4.1.0.beta1-released.html). Traditional binary gems can contain builds for several Ruby versions, making users download code they do not need. The new format gives each smaller, Ruby-version-specific build an identity derived from its contents, allowing clients to select the appropriate artifact. On RubyGems.org, we [aligned upload validation with the client](https://github.com/rubygems/rubygems.org/pull/6844) and refined the compact-index metadata. Together, these changes prepare the registry and clients to distribute smaller native gems while retaining compatibility with existing packages.

### Added a global registration throttle

A burst of roughly 40,000 account-creation requests early in September put pressure on the database even though none created an account. We added a [global registration rate limit](https://github.com/rubygems/rubygems.org/pull/6834) to bound the amount of signup traffic reaching the application, protecting the service from aggregate abuse. We also [corrected rejected registrations to return an HTTP error](https://github.com/rubygems/rubygems.org/pull/6832) instead of a successful response. The changes address both the load itself and the misleading signals that made failed registration attempts harder to distinguish from successful ones.

### Implemented OSV security advisory warnings on gem/version pages

We merged [OSV-backed security advisories on gem and version pages](https://github.com/rubygems/rubygems.org/pull/6814). The implementation identifies whether the version being viewed is affected, displays a warning, and links users to the advisory; affected releases are also marked in the version list. This brings vulnerability information into the place where developers already evaluate dependencies, helping them recognize an unsafe version without first knowing to consult a separate database. The implementation remains controlled by a feature flag.

## AI Security Engineers in Residence

September was a month focused fully on scanning, to capitalise on the 50% bonus offered by Anthropic. We ended up scanning 283 discrete gems with October planned for processing of these findings.

For August/September we have 52 validated findings (some of these were dismissed as invalid or low enough severity to not warrant a report), with 12 fixed, 5 pending fix validation, and 8 under report pending reply as of 30th September AEST.

[Kou](https://github.com/kou), our Ruby Core member, spent September validating and fixing the findings from his Scrutineer scans of Ruby and its bundled gems. He also verified two findings from the shared queue, a URI report that turned out not to be a vulnerability and an RDoc bug that had been reported before but only partly fixed, which he completed in [rdoc\#1832](https://github.com/ruby/rdoc/pull/1832). Of his own scan results, 3 of 20 Ruby findings and 1 of 13 REXML findings were verified, and none were vulnerabilities. He fixed them as ordinary bugs in Net::HTTP, Psych, and REXML so they won't later surface as false positive security reports. He also fixed a StringScanner out of bounds read and triaged several reports sent to Ruby Core, including two dRuby issues and a Marshal module hijacking report, all of which were classified as normal problems or feature requests rather than vulnerabilities. Still no critical vulnerabilities have been found, which is a good sign for the ecosystem.

### Harness work

We continue to be a major upstream contributor to the [Scrutineer](https://github.com/alpha-omega-security/scrutineer) scanning harness, [our contributions](https://github.com/alpha-omega-security/scrutineer/pulls?q=is%3Apr+state%3Aclosed+author%3Adkw-oss) span:

* Collaboration: Secure (encrypted) sharing of findings between handlers, Scrutineer was previously a single tenanted tool.  
* Scan quality: Proper support for monorepos and deep scanning of C language native extensions.  
* Cost tracking: Enables us to track operating costs of the program. While we currently operate under credits from Anthropic, this enables us to budget for the longer term lifecycle of the project when those cease.  
* Reporting: The Glasswing project has no structured metrics baseline, we have implemented one that can be used to track project health and performance over time for ourselves and any other users of the tool.  
* Auto-resume: Enable Scrutineer to resume when it hits Inference provider rate limits, allowing long running headless scan sessions.

These changes support a repeatable, measurable and cost-effective security-review process as scanning expands, while keeping sensitive findings protected.
